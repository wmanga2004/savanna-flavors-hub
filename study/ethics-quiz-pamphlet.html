Part B — Trickier / application questions

These match the kinds of questions you remembered. Answers follow the lecture framing.

B1

Cyberbullying happens because people use:

  • A. Internet
  • B. Cell phones
  • C. Television / radio
  • D. A & B are correct
  • E. All are correct

Answer

D. A & B are correct

Cyberbullying is harassment delivered through digital personal-communication tools — mainly the internet and cell phones. Television and radio are one-to-many broadcast media, not the usual channels for peer cyberbullying, so C (and therefore E) is wrong.

B2

Who is associated with The Second Treatise of Government?

  • A. John Stuart Mill
  • B. Immanuel Kant
  • C. John Locke
  • D. Jeremy Bentham
  • E. Thomas Hobbes

Answer

C. John Locke

The IP slides state: John Locke (1632–1704), The Second Treatise of Government. He argued people have rights to property in their person, their labor, and things removed from Nature through labor (with limits). Mill is linked to the harm principle / anti-censorship; Kant to the Categorical Imperative.

B3

The limitation of a small screen / awkward browsing on cell phones is mainly a problem that led to the rise of:

  • A. Spam filters
  • B. Mobile apps
  • C. Creative Commons
  • D. Trade secrets
  • E. Botnets

Answer

B. Mobile apps

Slides: people spend more time on smartphones/tablets; using Web browsers is awkward on mobile devices; mobile apps replace Web browsers as the way to access the Internet on mobile devices. Small frame size is a usability limit of the mobile Web, not of spam, IP licensing, or botnets.

B4

Which of the following best describes a site where people can trade goods or find work / services online?

  • A. Wikipedia
  • B. Twitter
  • C. Craigslist
  • D. Linux
  • E. Flickr Creative Commons gallery

Answer

C. Craigslist

Under “How We Use the Internet,” the slides highlight buying and selling. Craigslist is the classic classifieds marketplace for goods, housing, and work/gigs. Wikipedia is contributing content; Twitter is socializing/promotion; Linux is open-source software; Flickr CC is photo licensing/reuse.

B5

According to John Locke’s property argument (as applied in the slides), people have a natural right to things they:

  • A. Find abandoned on the Web
  • B. Remove from Nature through their own labor
  • C. Download after copyright expires
  • D. Copy under fair use for commercial sale
  • E. Patent as software algorithms only

Answer

B. Remove from Nature through their own labor

Locke: rights to property in one’s person, one’s labor, and things removed from Nature through labor — provided you don’t take more than you can use and enough is left for others. The slides then ask whether that extends cleanly to intellectual property (analogy is imperfect).

Internet, spam, mobile apps & censorship

Mostly Part A style — straight from ethic2.1.

1

Spam is best defined as:

  • A. Any work email
  • B. Unsolicited, bulk email
  • C. Encrypted email
  • D. Email with attachments
  • E. Newsletter you opted into

Answer

B. Unsolicited, bulk email

Slide definition: Spam = unsolicited, bulk email. Opt-in newsletters are solicited, so they are not spam under this definition.

2

Why is spam profitable?

  • A. It is illegal everywhere, raising prices
  • B. It costs about the same as paper junk mail
  • C. Sending ads by email is extremely cheap compared with postal junk mail
  • D. Recipients must pay to open spam
  • E. Governments subsidize spam

Answer

C. Sending ads by email is extremely cheap compared with postal junk mail

Slides: about $10 to email a million addresses; more than 30,000× cheaper than junk mail. Low cost + huge volume = profit incentive.

3

Most spam is sent by:

  • A. Individual hobbyists typing one email at a time
  • B. Bot herders who control botnets (networks of compromised computers)
  • C. The U.S. Postal Service
  • D. Creative Commons
  • E. Patent trolls

Answer

B. Bot herders who control botnets

Slides: most spam is sent by bot herders controlling huge networks of compromised computers (botnets).

4

Two important attributes of the World Wide Web are that it is:

  • A. Centralized and anonymous only
  • B. Decentralized, and every Web object has a unique address (URL)
  • C. Owned by one company and password-protected
  • D. Offline-first and print-only
  • E. Limited to government sites

Answer

B. Decentralized, and every Web object has a unique address (URL)

Direct from slides: Web is a networked hypertext system; two important attributes = decentralized + unique URL for every object.

5

Censorship is:

  • A. Always identical to copyright
  • B. An attempt to suppress or regulate public access to material considered harmful or offensive
  • C. Only done by private citizens
  • D. The same as fair use
  • E. Required by the 1st Amendment

Answer

B. Attempt to suppress or regulate public access to material considered harmful or offensive

Slide definition. Traditionally exercised by governments and religious institutions.

6

Which is an example of direct censorship?

  • A. A magazine decides not to publish to avoid angering officials
  • B. Movie ratings advising audiences
  • C. Government monopolization of all media outlets
  • D. A blogger choosing kinder wording
  • E. Fair use of a thumbnail image

Answer

C. Government monopolization of all media outlets

Direct censorship forms listed: government monopolization, prepublication review, licensing/registration. A and B are closer to self-censorship / ratings systems.

7

The most common form of censorship is:

  • A. Government monopolization
  • B. Prepublication review
  • C. Self-censorship
  • D. Licensing of radio only
  • E. Blocking all URLs worldwide

Answer

C. Self-censorship

Slides say self-censorship is the most common form: a group decides not to publish, often to avoid persecution or keep good relations with officials.

8

Which challenge makes Internet censorship especially hard?

  • A. There is only one Web site
  • B. Many-to-many communications, dynamic connections, huge number of sites, global reach
  • C. The Internet has no URLs
  • D. Minors and adults are always easy to distinguish online
  • E. All traffic is offline

Answer

B. Many-to-many, dynamic, huge, global

Slides list those challenges and also note it is hard to distinguish minors from adults online.

9

John Stuart Mill’s Principle of Harm says power may be exercised over a person against their will only:

  • A. To improve their moral character
  • B. To prevent harm to others
  • C. To protect intellectual property forever
  • D. To enforce religious doctrine
  • E. Whenever spam volume rises

Answer

B. To prevent harm to others

Quoted on slides: one’s own good, physical or moral, is not sufficient warrant. Mill also opposed censorship because no one is infallible and truth emerges from clash of ideas.

10

Freedom of expression in the U.S. is protected primarily by:

  • A. The 2nd Amendment
  • B. The 1st Amendment
  • C. The Audio Home Recording Act
  • D. Creative Commons only
  • E. Patent law

Answer

B. The 1st Amendment

Slides quote the 1st Amendment: Congress shall make no law abridging freedom of speech or of the press. It is not absolute — balanced against public good.

11

M-PESA (Kenya) is an example of text messaging / mobile tech used to:

  • A. Replace all universities
  • B. Save money, pay bills, transfer funds, share crop prices, fight counterfeit medicine
  • C. Enforce software patents
  • D. Host Creative Commons photos
  • E. Run botnets

Answer

B. Financial and practical uses listed in the slides

Under “Text Messaging / Transforming Lives in Developing Countries,” M-PESA enables saving, paying bills, transferring funds, crop-price info, and anti-counterfeit medicine protection.

Intellectual property, Locke & fair use

From ethic2.2 — strong Part A material; also supports Part B Locke questions.

12

Intellectual property refers to:

  • A. Land and buildings only
  • B. Creations of the mind (inventions, literary/artistic works, symbols/names/images used in commerce)
  • C. Only physical products in a warehouse
  • D. Electricity and water rights
  • E. Spam filters

Answer

B. Creations of the mind…

WIPO-style definition on slides. Important: IP ≠ the physical object that manifests it.

13

A trade secret is:

  • A. A public patent document
  • B. A confidential piece of IP that gives a company a competitive advantage
  • C. Always illegal reverse engineering
  • D. A Creative Commons license
  • E. A trademark slogan only

Answer

B. Confidential IP giving competitive advantage

Examples: formulas, processes, designs, plans, customer lists. Reverse engineering is allowed; secrets may leak when employees leave.

14

A trademark identifies _____; a service mark identifies _____.

  • A. services; goods
  • B. goods; services
  • C. patents; copyrights
  • D. spam; botnets
  • E. source code; binaries only

Answer

B. goods; services

Trademark = word/symbol/etc. identifying goods (Big Mac, iPhone). Service mark identifies services (Nike’s “Just Do It,” McDonald’s arches as brand identity examples on slides).

15

A U.S. patent typically gives the owner exclusive rights for about:

  • A. 5 years
  • B. 14 days
  • C. 20 years
  • D. Forever with no limits
  • E. Until the inventor dies only

Answer

C. 20 years

Slides: patent is a public document describing the invention; owner can prevent others from making/using/selling it for 20 years. Society later benefits when works enter the public domain.

16

Copyright’s five principal rights include all EXCEPT:

  • A. Reproduction
  • B. Distribution
  • C. Public display / performance
  • D. Production of derivative works
  • E. Permanent monopoly over mathematical truths

Answer

E. Permanent monopoly over mathematical truths

The five rights listed: reproduction, distribution, public display, public performance, derivative works. Copyright is limited in time/scope; it does not own “math truths.”

17

Courts judging fair use consider four factors. Which is one of them?

  • A. The author’s favorite color
  • B. Purpose/character of use, nature of work, amount copied, effect on the market
  • C. Whether the copier owns a smartphone
  • D. Whether spam filters blocked the file
  • E. Whether Locke wrote the work

Answer

B. The four fair-use factors

Listed exactly on slides. Fair use = sometimes legal to reproduce copyrighted work without permission under those circumstances.

18

In Sony v. Universal, the Supreme Court held that time shifting TV with a VCR is:

  • A. Always criminal
  • B. Fair use
  • C. A patent violation
  • D. Trademark infringement
  • E. Only legal for commercials

Answer

B. Fair use

Time shifting = recording to watch later privately. Using taped material for a commercial purpose is not fair use.

19

Space shifting (e.g., Rio MP3 player case) means:

  • A. Selling copies to strangers online
  • B. Creating a copy for backup or use on a portable device (generally treated as fair use)
  • C. Moving a data center to another country to avoid all law
  • D. Broadcasting radio without a license
  • E. Changing a trademark color

Answer

B. Backup / portable-device copy as fair use

RIAA v. Diamond Multimedia: space shifting consistent with copyright law. Making a copy for a friend is not fair use.

20

In Kelly v. Arriba Soft, thumbnail images in a search engine were:

  • A. Ruled never transformative
  • B. Affirmed as significantly transformative and fair use
  • C. Ruled to be patents
  • D. Banned worldwide
  • E. Treated as trade secrets

Answer

B. Significantly transformative and fair use

9th Circuit affirmed Arriba Soft’s thumbnails were fair use; later used as precedent in Authors Guild v. Google.

21

Why does society limit IP protection instead of making it permanent?

  • A. Creators never want incentives
  • B. Society benefits most when inventions eventually enter the public domain; limited rights balance incentive and public benefit
  • C. Patents are free and instant
  • D. Locke banned all property
  • E. Spam filters require it

Answer

B. Balance incentive vs. public domain

Slides: rights stimulate creativity, but society benefits when works enter public domain; Congress struck a time-limited compromise. Prices fall when works become public domain.

Case studies (Ann, Kate, Rajiv)

Application questions — common Part B / exam style.

22

Ann emails all 50 coworkers about Girl Scout cookies. Under rule utilitarianism, her action is judged:

  • A. Right, because one person sold cookies
  • B. Wrong, because if everyone solicited donations by email, morale would fall and causes would do poorly
  • C. Irrelevant to ethics
  • D. Required by Locke
  • E. A patent issue

Answer

B. Wrong under rule utilitarianism

Ask: what if everyone did this? Grumbling, lower morale, weaker results → harms > benefits. (Act utilitarianism in the slide’s numbers said benefits exceeded harms; theories disagree.)

23

A less controversial approach for Ann would be to:

  • A. Spam the whole Internet
  • B. Post a sign-up sheet and email only interested people (opt-in)
  • C. Hide her identity
  • D. Sell a fraudulent product
  • E. Force coworkers to buy

Answer

B. Opt-in via sign-up sheet

Summary slide: identify interested people first; avoid grumbling and lost productivity; also involve her daughter.

24

Kate posts Jerry’s party photo without permission. Kantian analysis says this is wrong because:

  • A. It increased Jerry’s popularity
  • B. She treated Jerry merely as a means to grow her blog’s readership
  • C. Utilitarianism always forbids blogging
  • D. Photos cannot be copyrighted
  • E. The 1st Amendment bans blogs

Answer

B. Treated him merely as a means

2nd formulation of the Categorical Imperative: respect autonomy; don’t use people only as means. Four of five analyses said she was wrong; she should have asked consent.

25

For Rajiv (database optimizations from a former employer), which option do Kantianism, social contract theory, and virtue ethics prefer?

  • A. Personally ask leading questions so the team rediscovers his old secrets
  • B. Clean-room strategy: public info + performance targets, no disclosing confidential optimizations
  • C. Publish the old employer’s source code
  • D. Sell the trade secret on Craigslist
  • E. Ignore all agreements

Answer

B. Clean-room strategy

Clean room doesn’t violate the confidentiality agreement and can be universalized; personally leading the team risks disclosing proprietary info (breaking a promise). Act utilitarianism’s dollar analysis preferred personal involvement, but a risk-averse view still favors clean room.

Software protection & open source

High-yield Part A facts; good for “which is true” items.

26

Software copyright protects:

  • A. The abstract idea only, not the expression
  • B. Expression/implementation (e.g., executable, screen displays), not the idea itself
  • C. Only the company’s building
  • D. Any mathematical algorithm forever
  • E. User passwords exclusively

Answer

B. Expression/implementation, not the idea

Slides: expression is copyrighted; companies often keep source as a trade secret. Object/executable programs are copyrightable (Apple v. Franklin).

27

Compared with software copyrights, software patents are generally:

  • A. Cheaper and faster to obtain; reverse engineering always allowed
  • B. More expensive and time-consuming; reverse engineering not allowed
  • C. Identical in every way
  • D. Illegal in all countries
  • E. Only for fonts

Answer

B. More expensive/time-consuming; reverse engineering not allowed

From the comparison table: copyright protects executable/screens; patent protects a software process with practical utility. Reverse engineering: yes for copyright, no for patent.

28

“Patent trolls” in the slides are described as:

  • A. Open-source maintainers
  • B. Companies that buy patents and enforce rights, often expecting settlement rather than trial
  • C. Spam filter vendors
  • D. Fair-use librarians
  • E. Girl Scout troops

Answer

B. Patent-holding companies enforcing patents for settlement leverage

Also called patent-holding companies; example pressure: RIM paid $612 million after not settling quickly.

29

Which is a feature of open-source software (per the open-source definition on slides)?

  • A. Source code must be secret forever
  • B. No one may modify the code
  • C. Source included; others may modify/redistribute; same rights apply to redistributions (copyleft-style sharing of rights)
  • D. Only the government may use it
  • E. It cannot be given away

Answer

C. Source included; modification/redistribution allowed; rights travel with redistributions

Also: no restrictions preventing selling/giving away; no restrictions on how people use software. Examples: Apache, Linux, Firefox, Android, etc.

30

Creative Commons licenses are meant to:

  • A. Replace the need for any copyright law overnight
  • B. Streamline creative reuse by stating which copying is OK and which rights are retained
  • C. Make all software patented
  • D. Ban Flickr
  • E. Legalize spam

Answer

B. Clarify allowed copying / retained rights

Works are copyrighted when created; contacting owners slows reuse. CC labels permissions. Flickr has hundreds of millions of CC-licensed photos (per slides).

31

Richard Stallman began the GNU Project in 1984 to:

  • A. Build a proprietary Windows clone for sale only
  • B. Develop an open-source, Unix-like operating system
  • C. Create the first botnet
  • D. Patent Amazon’s 1-Click
  • E. Run Acme’s payroll

Answer

B. Open-source Unix-like OS

Linus Torvalds wrote a Unix-like kernel in 1991; combined with GNU components → commonly called Linux.

32

Safe software development against unconscious copying uses a “clean room” where:

  • A. One team copies source line-by-line in secret
  • B. Team 1 analyzes a competitor’s program and writes a spec; Team 2 builds from the spec only
  • C. Everyone shares trade secrets freely
  • D. Patents are ignored
  • E. Reverse engineering is banned in all cases

Answer

B. Spec team vs. implementation team separation

Reverse engineering can be okay; clean room protects against unconscious copying of protected expression.
